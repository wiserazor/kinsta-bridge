What WordPress Hosting Really Costs

Every hosting advert quotes the cheapest month of the cheapest term. The number that decides your budget is the one printed underneath in smaller type: the renewal rate. This page puts both side by side for five hosts, then rolls them into three-year totals — because "cheap" and "not expensive over time" are two different questions.

Why the price "changes" after year one

None of this is a trick exactly, but it is designed to be skimmed past. Four mechanisms do most of the work:

  • The first-term discount. SiteGround's entry plan is advertised at 83% off; hosting.com's is 55% to 72% off depending on tier. The crossed-out number is not a competitor's price — it is the same host's standard rate.
  • The prepay requirement. The discount is attached to a twelve-month term paid upfront. SiteGround and hosting.com both work this way, so the cheap month is really a cheap year you pay in one go.
  • Renewal equals the standard rate. On hosting.com the monthly rate and the renewal rate are literally the same number ($9.99 / $17.99 / $25.99 / $34.99), which is unusually transparent. On SiteGround the intro rate expires and the standard rate takes over ($2.99 becomes $17.99).
  • Annual billing as a standing discount. Kinsta charges ten months for a twelve-month term, and that stays true at renewal. WP Engine discounts annual billing by 20% on its entry tier, but its headline price applies to new customers in year one only.

The hosts with no discount at all — Cloudways and, at the annual rate, Kinsta — look expensive in month one and predictable in month thirteen. That predictability is a feature you can price: over three years, Kinsta's Launch tier costs $1,050 with no surprises, while SiteGround's entry tier costs $467.64 and hosting.com's Small costs $293.63. Whether the difference is worth it depends on what the site earns, which is the next section's job.

How to read a hosting price page

Five checks turn a pricing page into a number you can budget against:

  • Match the toggle to your term. Annual and monthly views show different prices, and the annual view often displays a monthly-equivalent figure rather than the amount you actually pay today. Read the line that starts with "Pay".
  • Find the renewal sentence. Look for "Renews at", "then $X/mo on renewal", or "first year pricing". If a host doesn't publish it, that is itself information.
  • Do the multiplication yourself. Kinsta: monthly rate × 10 gives the annual total, always. SiteGround and hosting.com: the annual figure is the monthly-equivalent × 12, prepaid. If your own arithmetic disagrees with the page, trust the page and re-read it.
  • Count what isn't in the price. Additional sites, extra disk, object caching, backup retention beyond two weeks, email, staging and visits or bandwidth overages are all billed separately by at least one host here.
  • Check for first-year-only terms. A discount limited to new customers on one tier is not a price, it is a promotion — and it won't apply when you upgrade.

hosting.com — the cheapest entry, and it tells you the renewal

hosting.com's WordPress plans are the cheapest entry point of the five, and unusually for this category it publishes the renewal rate next to the intro price instead of burying it. On a prepaid year the monthly-equivalent rates are $4.49, $4.99, $10.99 and $14.99 for Small, Medium, Advanced and Turbo; switch the toggle to monthly and you see exactly what those plans renew at: $9.99, $17.99, $25.99 and $34.99.

One consequence is worth acting on. Small's first year costs $53.87 and Medium's costs $59.89 — six dollars apart — while Medium carries four times the visitor allowance, five installs instead of one, and a renewal ($17.99) that is under twice Small's ($9.99). For anything beyond a single small site, the second tier is the better buy from day one, and that is visible only because the renewal rates are published.

Every plan includes unlimited PHP workers, a 99.99% uptime guarantee, free SSL, their Enterprise CDN, a web application firewall, malware protection, free migrations, daily backups with 14-day retention and staging. WP Rocket and Object Cache Pro are bundled from Medium upward; Relay, their in-memory cache, is Turbo-only.

Cloudways — no discount, no jump

Cloudways rents you a server on a cloud provider you choose and manages the stack. There is no first-term discount, which means the price you see is the price you keep paying: the DigitalOcean entry tier is $11/month for 2 GB RAM, 1 vCPU, 50 GB storage and 2 TB of bandwidth, and it stays $11 in year three. Servers bill hourly too, so a short project doesn't pay for a full month.

That flat structure makes Cloudways the easiest of the five to budget for, and its hosting bill is the second-lowest three-year total in the table above. The trade-offs show up elsewhere: bandwidth overages are billed per GB ($0.40 per 10 GB), extra disk is $2 per GB, and you carry more of the WordPress layer yourself — plugin conflicts and some tuning are your problem, not a support ticket. The 8 GB tier is $88/month and the largest published DigitalOcean configuration is $342/month.

Kinsta — ten months for twelve

Kinsta's pricing is the least promotional of the five. The Business line runs Launch, Launch Plus, Build, Expand, Scale and Lead at $35, $59, $69, $99, $169 and $299 per month; annual billing charges ten months for a twelve-month term, so Launch is $350 a year whether it is your first year or your fifth. Agency tiers are sized by install count — 20, 40 and 60 installs at $340, $450 and $675 per month — and Enterprise is quote-based from $500/month.

So Kinsta never springs a renewal increase, and it also never gives you a cheap first year. What it charges for is the structure: isolated containers on Google Cloud, a per-site dashboard, engineer-level support, and visits that are metered and billed rather than throttled. Its add-ons are where budgets drift: an additional site is $30/month, an extra 20 GB of disk is $20/month, and object caching (Redis) is a paid addition rather than a bundled feature.

WP Engine — the renewal it doesn't publish

WP Engine's pricing page now leads with three tiers: Essential, Core and Enterprise. Essential is $336 per year billed annually, which works out at about $28/month, and additional sites cost $20/month each. Core and Enterprise are quote-based.

The reason WP Engine sits at the bottom of the table with "unknown" rather than a total is that its headline figure is explicitly first-year pricing for new customers, and the page says renewal pricing is subject to change. That is a legitimate way to sell, but it means the second year of an Essential plan cannot be computed from public information — by the host's own terms, not our estimate. If you are budgeting three years out, ask for the renewal rate in writing before you commit.

SiteGround — the classic sixfold renewal

SiteGround is the clearest example of the pattern this page exists to expose. StartUp is advertised at $2.99/month, GrowBig at $4.99 and GoGeek at $7.99 — all requiring a twelve-month prepay, all discounted 82% or 83% from the standard rate. The renewal line underneath says $17.99, $29.99 and $44.99.

Nothing there is hidden: it is printed on the same card as the discount. But the ratio is easy to miss. StartUp's second year costs more than five times its first, which is why its three-year total ($467.64) overtakes hosting.com's Medium tier ($491.65) only narrowly despite starting $24 cheaper in year one — and overtakes hosting.com's Small tier ($293.63) outright. The same structure applies to GrowBig ($779.64 over three years) and GoGeek ($1,175.64).

What SiteGround does include is real: free email, CDN, SSL, backups, dynamic caching and memcached across all three plans, plus one-click staging and priority support on GoGeek. Judge it on the renewal rate, not the advert.

Four ways to pay less without switching hosts

  • Buy the tier you need now, not the tier you hope to need. Where the ladder is steeply discounted at the bottom (hosting.com is the extreme case: $53.87 for Small versus $59.89 for Medium in year one), moving up a tier early costs a few dollars and buys several times the capacity — while moving up mid-term means paying the standard rate for the difference.
  • Prepay only when the discount is real, and check the year-three number before you do. A prepay that saves $200 in year one and costs $200 more in year two is a loan, not a discount. Run both terms through the calculator above.
  • Price the add-ons before you upgrade a tier. Additional sites run $20/month at WP Engine and $30/month at Kinsta; extra disk is $20 per 20 GB at Kinsta and $2 per GB at Cloudways; Redis is a paid addition at Kinsta and included with the server at Cloudways. Sometimes the add-on is cheaper than the tier, and sometimes the tier is cheaper than the add-ons.
  • Watch the two meters, not the plan name. Visits (Kinsta) and bandwidth (Cloudways) are the two things billed beyond the plan, and both hosts publish the overage rate. A plan that fits your traffic costs what it says; a plan that doesn't is the most expensive way to buy hosting.

FAQ

Why did my WordPress hosting price go up after the first year?

Because the first-year price was a discount, not a rate. Most hosts in this category sell the first term below cost to win the account and then bill the standard rate from renewal onward. On SiteGround the entry plan goes from $2.99 to $17.99 per month; on hosting.com from a $4.49 monthly equivalent to $9.99. Hosts that never discount, such as Cloudways, don't have a second-year jump because there was nothing to expire.

Is it cheaper to pay monthly or yearly?

Yearly, on almost every host, but the size of the saving varies wildly. Kinsta gives you ten months for twelve at any tier, so the annual term saves about 17%. SiteGround's and hosting.com's headline rates require a prepaid year and save far more than that — which is exactly why the renewal rate matters more than the discount. Cloudways charges the same either way and bills hourly.

Which of these hosts is cheapest over three years?

On entry plans, hosting.com's Small tier at $293.63, followed by Cloudways' 2 GB server at $396. SiteGround's StartUp overtakes both in year three despite the cheapest first invoice, because its renewal is six times its intro rate. Kinsta is predictable but higher at $1,050 for three years of Launch. WP Engine cannot be ranked, because it publishes first-year pricing and says renewal pricing is subject to change.

Can I avoid the renewal increase?

Not on a host that discounts, short of switching hosts or moving to a longer prepaid term. The workable strategies are to compare renewal rates rather than intro prices, to choose a host whose standard rate you can live with, and to time a migration to the end of a discounted term rather than the middle of it. Asking support for a loyalty rate sometimes works, but it is not something to budget on.

Do these prices include tax, email and backups?

No on tax: all five quote USD prices excluding tax, and the amount depends on your billing country. Email is included by hosting.com and SiteGround and not by Kinsta, WP Engine or Cloudways. Backups are included everywhere, but retention differs — 14 days at Kinsta and hosting.com, and longer retention is a paid add-on on most tiers.

What happens if I exceed my visits or bandwidth?

It depends which meter you exceed. Kinsta meters visits and bills overages rather than throttling your site. Cloudways bills bandwidth per GB beyond the plan, $0.40 per 10 GB, and extra disk at $2 per GB. SiteGround and hosting.com size plans by visitors and bandwidth as soft limits, which in practice means a forced upgrade conversation rather than a line item — worth knowing before a launch.
