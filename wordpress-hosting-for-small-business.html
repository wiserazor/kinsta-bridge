What a business owner actually needs

You don't need to know what a PHP worker is. You need four things to be true without thinking about them:

  • The site is up. Managed hosting handles security patching, backups, and uptime monitoring so you're not the one discovering an outage from an angry customer.
  • Support answers in plain English. When something breaks, you want a human who fixes WordPress — not a chatbot that pastes a knowledge-base link.
  • Moving is painless. Most small businesses are stuck on oversold shared hosting (often GoDaddy or a cPanel box). A good managed host migrates you in for free.
  • The bill is predictable. No surprise overages, no "renewal" doubling after year one that you didn't see coming.

That's the whole checklist. Everything else — CDN, staging, edge caching — is a bonus. Let's see how the five hosts score on it.

Moving from GoDaddy / cPanel

Most small businesses start on a cheap shared plan and stay because moving feels risky. It usually isn't. All five managed hosts offer free migrations, and the rough sequence is:

  • Sign up for the new host (most have a money-back window — test the dashboard and support first).
  • Hand over your current credentials or run their migration plugin; they clone the site.
  • Review the clone on a staging URL — click around, check forms and the contact page.
  • Flip DNS to point the domain at the new host. Downtime is typically a few minutes during propagation.
  • Keep the old plan running a week as a fallback, then cancel.

The biggest myth is "I'll lose my email." If your email is on the same provider as your old hosting, ask the new host or your domain registrar how to keep mail flowing — it's a configuration step, not a reason to stay stuck.

What managed hosting actually costs

The number on the pricing page is the beginning of the maths, not the end of it. Three things routinely surprise business owners:

  • Intro price ≠ renewal price. Budget shared hosting in particular discounts the first term heavily and renews higher. Always read the renewal rate before you compare — a $3/mo plan that renews at $15/mo is a $15/mo plan.
  • The cheap plan excludes the thing you'll need. Daily backups, staging, email, and object caching are sometimes bundled and sometimes metered add-ons. Two hosts at the same sticker price can differ meaningfully once you add what your business actually uses.
  • Your time is a line item. On unmanaged or oversold hosting, you (or whoever you pay) become the sysadmin: updates, security patches, "why is the site slow," recovery after a bad plugin. That cost doesn't appear on any invoice.

Here's the rough split of what's usually bundled versus what's commonly billed separately. Treat it as a checklist to verify with each host, not gospel — inclusions change and vary by plan tier:

Typically includedOften an add-on or tier-dependent
SSL certificate, CDN, automatic WordPress core updates, some form of daily backup, staging on most managed plans Email hosting, extended backup retention, object caching (Redis), additional visits or bandwidth, premium support tiers, higher PHP-worker allocation

A useful way to frame the decision: compare the annual hosting cost against the value of a single lost enquiry. If a slow or down site costs you one good lead a month, the gap between a $10 plan and a $30 plan has already paid for itself. If your site is a static brochure that generates nothing directly, that same gap is just overhead — and it's perfectly reasonable to stay cheap.

Kinsta for business

For a business where the website earns money — bookings, leads, sales — Kinsta's pitch is predictability. The relevant points:

  • Isolated containers on Google Cloud C2 mean a traffic spike elsewhere never slows your site.
  • Free white-glove migrations bring you over including a staging check before DNS cutover.
  • 24/7 engineer-level support — when the site is down at 9 p.m., the person on the other end fixes WordPress.
  • Automatic daily backups with one-click restore, so a bad plugin update isn't a catastrophe.

Pros: Reliable origin performance; genuine isolation; strong support; painless migration; easy restores.

Cons: Higher entry price than budget hosts; no bundled email; visit caps need light monitoring.

Cloudways, WP Engine, SiteGround & hosting.com for business

Cloudways

Cloudways is the "control" option: you pick the cloud (DigitalOcean, AWS, Google Cloud) and they manage the stack. It makes the most sense for a business that has — or can afford — someone technical, and wants server-level control without building the stack from scratch. The entry price is attractive; the learning curve is the real cost.

Business-relevant notes

  • Lower entry cost than fully managed, with the flexibility to resize the server as the business grows.
  • Free migration tooling is available, though it's more self-serve than the white-glove service premium hosts offer.
  • Metered bandwidth: model your traffic before committing — a busy month can cost more than the sticker price suggests.
  • You own more of the WordPress-level tuning, updates, and troubleshooting.

Pros: Lower entry cost; choose your cloud; flexible scaling; free migration tool.

Cons: Steeper learning curve without a developer; bandwidth overages can surprise; more tuning on you.

WP Engine

WP Engine targets larger, brand-name businesses with compliance and governance needs. For a typical small business it's usually more platform than necessary — you'd be paying for process and procurement features you won't use. It becomes the right answer when a client, insurer, or regulator is asking questions about how the site is run.

Business-relevant notes

  • Governance and accountability: the differentiator is process, not raw speed.
  • Plugin policy: some plugins are restricted — check your must-haves against their list before migrating.
  • Mature support and staging suited to teams with formal release processes.

Pros: Enterprise trust; mature staging; strong governance; reliable platform.

Cons: Pricier at entry; stricter plugin policies; often overkill for a simple brochure or lead-gen site.

SiteGround

SiteGround is the friendly on-ramp: cheap to start, fast onboarding, and genuinely good support — which matters a lot when you're non-technical and something breaks. It's the sane default for a first business site. The two things to plan for are shared resources as you grow, and the renewal rate once the intro discount ends.

Business-relevant notes

  • Support quality is the standout — reachable humans who know WordPress.
  • Free migration gets you off oversold shared hosting without paying a developer.
  • Read the renewal price before you sign; the intro rate is temporary.
  • Shared resources: fine early, a constraint once traffic becomes meaningful.

Pros: Lowest first-term cost; excellent support; beginner-friendly; free migration.

Cons: Shared resources (noisy-neighbor risk as you grow); renewal prices jump significantly.

hosting.com

hosting.com — the former A2 Hosting, rebranded in 2026 — is the budget-performance option. Its LiteSpeed/Turbo stack delivers strong speed for the money, and it undercuts every fully-managed host here on entry price. The trade-off is hand-holding: you get a fast server and good developer tooling, and you take on more of the day-to-day WordPress care yourself.

Business-relevant notes

  • Speed for the price is the headline — LiteSpeed/Turbo tiers perform well on cached requests.
  • Developer tooling (SSH, staging, WP-CLI) is part of the package rather than an upsell.
  • Verify what your tier includes: backup retention, staging, and resource allocation vary by plan — confirm before assuming parity with managed hosts.

Pros: Strong performance per pound/dollar; low entry cost; developer tooling included.

Cons: Less hand-holding than fully managed; inclusions vary by tier — verify backups and resource limits; you own more of the upkeep.

Tip: Whatever you pick, turn on automatic backups and make sure you know where the restore button is before you need it. The businesses that survive a bad update are the ones with a one-click rollback, not the ones with a frantic developer on speed dial.

FAQ

Do I need to be technical to use managed hosting?

No. That's the entire point. Staging, backups, SSL, and PHP switching are one-click in the dashboard. If you can use a web browser, you can run a managed WordPress site.

Will moving hosts break my site?

It shouldn't. Free migrations clone the site to a staging URL first so you can verify everything works before flipping DNS. Keep the old plan for a week as a safety net.

What about my email?

If email lives with your old host, coordinate mail separately during the move — it's a DNS setting, not a reason to stay. Many businesses move mail to Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 and host the site separately.

Is the cheap first-term price real?

The intro price is real but temporary. Budget hosts especially raise rates at renewal. Read the renewal price, not just the first-term price, when comparing.

Which host is "safest" for a non-technical owner?

The one whose support you can actually reach and whose backups restore in one click. All five qualify; the difference is price tier and how much control you want versus how much you want handled for you.

Do I need to buy email hosting from my web host?

No, and often you shouldn't. Many managed WordPress hosts deliberately don't bundle email — it's a different reliability problem than serving web pages. Most businesses are better off with a dedicated mail provider (Google Workspace or Microsoft 365) and web hosting separately, which also means moving hosts later doesn't touch your email at all. If email currently lives with your old host, plan that separation during the migration.

Is managed hosting worth it for a simple brochure site?

Often not. If the site exists so people can confirm you're real and find your phone number, a premium managed plan is money you could spend elsewhere — a cheap shared plan with decent support is rational. Managed hosting earns its premium when the site does something: captures leads, takes bookings, sells products, or is slow enough to cost you customers. Upgrade when the site starts earning, not before.

What should I check before I sign up?

Five things: the renewal price (not the intro price); whether backups are included and how far back they go; whether staging is available on your tier; what happens when you exceed visits or bandwidth; and the refund window so you can test support and the dashboard before you're committed. Most hosting regret comes from skipping one of these.
